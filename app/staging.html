What this measures

How faint a striped pattern can you still see, at five bar sizes?

Each trial shows a circular patch of parallel bars tilted left, straight up, or tilted right. You pick the tilt. Bars get fainter down each column and narrower across the five columns (A–E, 1.5 to 18 cycles per degree). The faintest patch you still identify at each bar size gives your contrast sensitivity curve, one per eye.

Screening only. This page does not diagnose anything. Low scores can come from screen limits, uncorrected vision, cataract, fatigue, or many other causes. Discuss results with an optometrist or doctor who knows contrast sensitivity testing.

Step 1 of 3

Set up screen size and distance

Spatial frequency is measured in cycles per degree of visual angle, so the page needs your pixel size and viewing distance. Hold a bank card against the screen and drag the slider until the dashed box matches it exactly. This sets size only; screen brightness and contrast are not measured.

Sit comfortably, measure eye to screen, and keep that distance for the whole test. 50 cm is typical for a laptop.

Step 2 of 3

Before you begin

  1. Set screen brightness to maximum and turn off night-shift or blue-light filters.
  2. Sit in a normally lit room with no glare on the screen.
  3. Wear the glasses or contacts you normally use for the screen.
  4. Cover one eye with your palm (do not press). The page tests the right eye first, then the left.
  5. For each patch, answer Left, Up or Right. Keyboard arrows work too. Guess if you are unsure; use Can't see only when there is no pattern at all.

Bars are tilted 15° from vertical, as in the FACT / VCTS charts. Patches start easy (column A, high contrast) and get harder.

Evidence status

Visual contrast sensitivity measures how faint a pattern can be discriminated across spatial frequencies. It depends on both optical factors (refractive error, clarity of the cornea and lens, pupil size, light scatter) and neural factors (retina, optic nerve and visual cortex), so reduced sensitivity can arise from ocular disease such as cataract, glaucoma or macular disease, from uncorrected refractive error, and from neurological conditions, medication, alcohol and fatigue. It was popularised by Shoemaker as a bedside marker of biotoxin-related illness after exposure to water-damaged buildings. The evidence is mixed and the test is not validated as a diagnostic tool for mould-related illness:

  • A NIOSH study found lower VCS values at every spatial frequency among employees of a severely water-damaged school compared with a comparison school, together with more work-related rash, respiratory and constitutional symptoms. The same investigators nevertheless advised against recommending VCS for evaluating mould-exposed people, citing non-representative samples, confounding by conditions such as fibromyalgia and chronic fatigue, missing comparison groups and poor exposure characterisation in earlier work [12].
  • A 2026 comparison of four methods found that the original handheld Shoemaker chart did not separate biotoxin-exposed people from controls, whereas three digital tests did, the best reaching 100% sensitivity at 60–80% specificity. Test method matters, and the digital tools remain investigational [13].
  • The American College of Medical Toxicology states that no validated biomarker exists for mould or mycotoxin exposure; fungal IgE/IgG testing bears only on allergic sensitisation [14]. A Lancet Infectious Diseases review notes there is no diagnostic test for building-related illness, which rests on history, exposure assessment and exclusion of other causes [15]; a proposed framework for dampness and mould hypersensitivity syndrome likewise uses clinical and exposure criteria rather than any laboratory or visual test [16].

In an Australian clinical context, VCS should be treated as non-validated and not a stand-alone diagnostic or monitoring tool. Suspected building-related illness is better approached through a thorough exposure history, exclusion of alternative causes (inflammatory markers, anaemia, liver, renal and thyroid function, chest imaging), assessment for fungal sensitisation where allergic or respiratory symptoms dominate, and remediation of the damp or mould source. The Hudnell 2001 cohort found visual deficits but could not attribute them to Pfiesteria exposure; reduced contrast sensitivity is also produced by refractive error, cataract, glaucoma, macular disease, diabetes, alcohol and many drugs.
