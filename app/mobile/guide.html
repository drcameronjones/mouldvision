Visual contrast sensitivity and the indoor mould exposome

Before you begin

Why this guide exists

If you have spent time in a water-damaged home, school or workplace and you are not feeling well, you have probably come across the visual contrast sensitivity (VCS) test. It is often described online as a "mould test for your eyes". The real picture is more interesting, and more useful, than that.

This guide reviews the published research on VCS and indoor mould exposure. It sets out where the idea came from, what the studies actually found, what independent reviewers and medical guideline groups have concluded, and what makes a contrast test trustworthy. It finishes with a practical, step-by-step approach for people who want to use a contrast test sensibly alongside a proper building assessment.

I have tried to write it the way I would explain the evidence to a client standing in a damp house: plainly, without hype, and without dismissing real concerns. Every factual claim is numbered and linked to its source in the reference list at the back.

The short version

  • Contrast sensitivity is a real, well-established measure of visual function. It picks up changes that a standard letter chart can miss.1,2
  • Small studies, mostly from one research group, have reported lower contrast sensitivity in people from water-damaged buildings.9,10 An independent NIOSH study and a 2026 Australian study also found group-level differences.11,13
  • No study has shown that a contrast test can tell whether an individual person has a mould-related illness. Reduced contrast sensitivity has many causes, from cataract to ageing to solvents.1,14,16
  • A major German-language medical guideline lists the VCS test among methods not to be used to assess indoor mould exposure, and NIOSH advised against using it to evaluate individuals.20,11
  • Used honestly, a contrast test is best treated as a personal tracking tool and a prompt for an eye examination, not a diagnosis. The building itself still needs to be investigated.29

Who this guide is for

People living or working in a damp or water-damaged building who want to understand what a contrast sensitivity test can and cannot tell them; health practitioners who are asked about VCS by patients; and building professionals who want a balanced summary to share with clients.

How to read the evidence in this guide

Studies are described with their design, size and limitations, not just their headline result. Where a finding comes from a single research group, a small sample or a non-randomised design, that is stated. Chapter 8 brings everything together as a simple grading: established, suggestive or not established.

This guide is general information, not medical advice. It does not diagnose or treat any condition. If you have sudden changes in vision, eye pain, flashes of light, a shadow or curtain across your vision, or double vision, seek same-day medical or optometric care.

Chapter 1

What contrast sensitivity is

A standard eye chart asks how small a black letter on a white background you can read. Contrast sensitivity asks a different question: how faint can a pattern become before you can no longer see it?

Most of the world is not high-contrast black on white. Faces in dim light, a kerb at dusk, a grey car in rain: everyday vision depends on detecting subtle differences in light and dark. Contrast sensitivity measures the minimum contrast a person needs to detect a target, and it differs from acuity, which measures resolving ability at very high contrast.1 Fechner reported as early as 1860 that threshold contrast is around 1% for a wide range of targets.2

Stripes, frequencies and the sensitivity curve

Many contrast tests use soft-edged patches of stripes called sine-wave gratings. Coarse, wide stripes have a low spatial frequency; fine, narrow stripes have a high spatial frequency. Frequency is expressed in cycles per degree (cpd) of visual angle, which is why viewing distance matters so much.

When sensitivity is plotted across frequencies, it forms an inverted-U shape called the contrast sensitivity function. People are most sensitive at middle frequencies (roughly 3 to 6 cpd) and less sensitive at very coarse and very fine patterns. The tests used in mould research sample five points along this curve: 1.5, 3, 6, 12 and 18 cpd.3,9

How contrast is expressed

Grating contrast is usually given as Michelson contrast: the difference between the brightest and darkest parts of the stripes divided by their sum. A 100% grating runs from black to white; a 1% grating is barely distinguishable from flat grey. Sensitivity is the reciprocal of the threshold contrast and is usually reported on a log scale, so a threshold of 1% equals a log sensitivity of 2.0.

Why clinicians care

Contrast sensitivity is a recognised clinical and research measure. It can be reduced by cataract, glaucoma, retinal disease and normal ageing, and these losses can affect daily tasks even when letter acuity is still good.1 The open question is whether it adds anything specific when the concern is mould.

Chapter 2

Where the mould and VCS idea came from

The link between contrast sensitivity and environmental illness did not start with mould. It started in the estuaries of the eastern United States in the late 1990s.

Outbreaks of the dinoflagellate Pfiesteria were associated with fish kills and with symptoms in people who worked on or near affected waterways, a cluster later described as possible estuary-associated syndrome (PEAS). Ken Hudnell, a neurotoxicologist at the US Environmental Protection Agency, used VCS as a quick field measure of neurological function in exposed people.4

In 2001, Shoemaker and Hudnell described cases with reported VCS deficits after estuary exposure, and Hudnell and colleagues reported lower VCS in a group of North Carolina watermen than in comparison subjects.5,6 Dr Ritchie Shoemaker later extended the approach to people living or working in water-damaged buildings, and VCS became one of the markers in the chronic inflammatory response syndrome (CIRS) framework that he developed.24,25

The debate began early

In 2002, two North Carolina physicians, Marian Swinker and William Burke, wrote to Environmental Health Perspectives challenging the use of VCS as a diagnostic tool. They argued that it is non-specific, had not been validated against standard neuropsychological tests, and that supporting papers often cited one another.7 A follow-up letter raised a further confounder: watermen spend years in bright sunlight, which is linked to eye disease that itself reduces contrast sensitivity.8

These two lines of thought, a promising functional marker on one side and concerns about specificity and validation on the other, have run in parallel ever since.

Chapter 3

The studies in water-damaged buildings

Only a handful of studies have tested VCS in people exposed to damp or mouldy buildings. Each is worth looking at closely, because the details matter more than the headlines.

What these studies tell us

Taken together, the studies suggest that groups of people from water-damaged buildings can show lower average contrast sensitivity than comparison groups. That is a genuine and repeated signal, and it deserves to be taken seriously.

They do not show that reduced contrast sensitivity is caused by mould specifically, that it can identify individual cases, or that a change in score proves a change in exposure. The NIOSH finding is particularly instructive: even in a building with a clear damp problem and a measurable group difference, most individual staff members had results in the normal range.11

An important Australian finding

The 2026 Australian study's most practical message is that the test method matters. The printed handheld chart traditionally used in CIRS clinics failed to tell exposed and unexposed people apart, while digital tests performed better. However, the commercial digital tests had low specificity, which means they flagged many of the unexposed controls.13 It is worth reading the original paper rather than a summary.

Chapter 4

Why a low score is not specific to mould

A test is only diagnostic if a particular result points to a particular cause. Contrast sensitivity responds to a long list of exposures, conditions and life stages.

Perchloroethylene (dry-cleaning solvent)Residents (n = 17) and day-care staff (n = 9) near dry-cleaners had lower VCS with unchanged acuity. A reanalysis of adults only found the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.16).Schreiber et al. 200214; Storm & Mazor 200415

This is not a weakness of contrast sensitivity as a measure. It is exactly what you would expect of a sensitive test of the visual pathway. But it means a low score is a question, not an answer. Before attributing a result to a building, the more common causes need to be ruled out, and that is the job of an optometrist or ophthalmologist.

The NIOSH authors made the same point: because many conditions affect contrast sensitivity, the test cannot single out mould exposure in an individual.11

Practical takeaway

If your contrast results are lower than you expect, book an eye examination first. Many of the conditions that reduce contrast sensitivity are treatable, and some, such as glaucoma, are time-sensitive.

Chapter 5

What guideline bodies and governments say

Expert groups that have reviewed the evidence agree on one central point: dampness and mould are a real health concern, especially for the airways. They diverge sharply from the CIRS view on VCS.

Respiratory effects are well established

The World Health Organization concluded in 2009 that there is sufficient evidence linking indoor dampness and mould with respiratory symptoms, asthma development and exacerbation, and respiratory infections.18 A major epidemiological review in 2011 reached similar conclusions.19 This is the strongest evidence base in the field, and it is the reason damp buildings must be fixed regardless of anything a vision test shows.

Positions on VCS

BodyPosition
AWMF mould guideline, Germany, Austria and Switzerland (update 2023, published 2024)Key message 25, a strong consensus of more than 95% of the panel, lists the VCS test among methods not to be used in the medical diagnosis of indoor mould exposure. The guideline rates the evidence linking indoor mould with sick building syndrome, multiple chemical sensitivity and chronic fatigue syndrome as inadequate or insufficient.20
NIOSH, United States (2012)Did not recommend VCS for evaluating people exposed to mould, citing non-specificity.11,12
American College of Medical Toxicology (2025)Allergic conditions, rather than toxic effects of inhaled mycotoxins, are the primary health concern. Cautions against unvalidated diagnostic tests and unproven "detox" treatments.21
CIRS-aligned clinicians (2024 to 2025)Recommend VCS as a screening tool and part of a multi-marker case definition.24,25

A note on the CIRS reviews

Two recent reviews argue that VCS should be used in primary care screening for CIRS.24,25 They are useful summaries of the CIRS position, but much of the evidence they draw on comes from one research group, and independent replication is still limited.

The Australian position

In 2018, the House of Representatives Standing Committee on Health, Aged Care and Sport reported on its inquiry into biotoxin-related illnesses. It made seven recommendations, including a public fact sheet on mould and health and further research.22 The Australian Government's response was published in March 2020.23

In other words, Australian policy accepts that people are unwell and that buildings matter, while calling for better research rather than endorsing specific diagnostic tests. The Australian Mould Guideline 2026 takes a building-first approach to investigating and remediating moisture and mould.29

Chapter 6

Reading a test result: accuracy, noise and probability

Two pieces of arithmetic explain why a single contrast score should never be read as a verdict.

1. Even good tests give many false alarms when used widely

Sensitivity is how often a test flags people who truly have a condition. Specificity is how often it correctly clears people who do not. What a person really wants to know is different: if my result is low, how likely is it that I actually have the condition? That depends on how common the condition is among the people taking the test.

Figure 3 works this through for two tests in three illustrative groups. Using the best performance reported in the 2026 Australian study, if 5 in 100 people taking the test truly had a biotoxin-related condition, only about 15% of low results would be true positives.13 For a less specific test, it falls to about 6%.

2. Every test has measurement noise

Studies of the printed grating charts have found their test–retest repeatability to be poor.3,26 On the nine-row ladder used by this test, each row is about 0.22 log units fainter than the one before. With the kind of noise reported for paper charts, a one-row change between two sessions can easily be chance.

The lesson is simple: look for consistent patterns across repeated sessions, not a single number.

Chapter 7

What makes a digital contrast test credible

Phones and computers can measure contrast sensitivity well, but only if the test controls the things that most affect the result.

Computer testing is increasingly popular because it allows efficient, adaptive threshold measurement, but it needs careful attention to calibration and procedure.2 Some smartphone tests have performed well: the Peek contrast sensitivity test, for example, showed high repeatability and good agreement with the Pelli-Robson chart.28 Performance varies between apps, and reviews recommend checking each one.26,27

The technical requirements

  • Viewing distance. Spatial frequency depends on how far away the screen is, so distance must be controlled.
  • Display luminance and gamma. Screens do not convert pixel values into brightness linearly, so contrast must be computed in real luminance.
  • Bit depth. A standard 8-bit display has only 256 grey levels. By the author's calculation (assuming a standard sRGB screen and a mid-grey background), a single grey-level step corresponds to roughly 0.8 to 1.7% Michelson contrast. Showing fainter patterns needs special techniques such as bit-stealing, which have their own limits.27
  • Forced choice. Asking "can you see it?" depends on how cautious the person is. Asking them to identify something, such as which way stripes tilt, is more objective.2
  • Conditions. Each eye separately, in stable lighting, with usual glasses, plus repeatability and normal ranges measured in the population that will use the test.

So, does the method have scientific merit?

Yes, within clear limits. Contrast sensitivity is sound vision science, and the group-level findings in damp buildings are a legitimate research signal. The merit lies in using it as a careful, repeatable personal measure of visual function, tracked over time and paired with an eye examination and a proper building assessment. It does not lie in using it to diagnose mould illness, and claims that it does go beyond the evidence.

Chapter 9

Using a contrast test responsibly: a practical plan

If you are concerned about a damp building and want to use a contrast test, this is the approach the evidence supports.

Step 1. Start with the building

Your health concern is a reason to investigate the building, not the other way around. Look for and record water leaks, condensation, musty odours, visible growth, past flooding and recent renovations. A building-first assessment of moisture, materials and air, as set out in the Australian Mould Guideline 2026, is what identifies and fixes the source.29 No eye test can tell you whether your house has a mould problem.

Step 2. Talk to your GP and have an eye examination

Respiratory symptoms, allergies and asthma are the best-established health effects of damp buildings and should be assessed by your GP.18 If you have any concern about your vision, an optometrist can check for cataract, glaucoma, retinal changes and other common causes of reduced contrast sensitivity.1

Step 3. If you track, set a baseline properly

  • Use the same device, screen brightness, room lighting and time of day each session.
  • Wear the glasses or contact lenses you normally use for reading, and test each eye separately.
  • Take two or three sessions on different days to establish your own baseline.
  • Avoid testing when very tired, unwell or straight after screen-heavy work.

Step 4. Read trends, not single results

Treat a single low result as a reason to repeat, not to worry. A change of one row between sessions can be ordinary test noise.3 A consistent change across several sessions, especially in one eye, is a reason to see an optometrist.

Step 5. Keep a simple log

Write down building events alongside your results: when leaks were found, when remediation started and finished, and when you moved in or out. This turns isolated numbers into a timeline you can discuss with your health practitioner and building consultant.

Step 6. Be wary of using scores to justify treatment

Medical toxicology and guideline bodies advise against basing treatment decisions on unvalidated tests.20,21 Discuss any proposed treatment with your GP.

Seek same-day care if you notice

Sudden loss or blurring of vision, a shadow or curtain across your vision, new flashes or floaters, double vision, or eye pain. These need urgent assessment and should never be monitored with a home test.

Chapter 10

What research would settle the question

The disagreement about VCS is ultimately a data problem. Better data would resolve it, in either direction.

The studies that would move the field forward are not complicated, but they are rarely done:

  • Normal ranges and repeatability for the specific test and device being used, across ages.
  • Blinded comparisons where the person scoring the test does not know who is exposed.
  • Measured exposure, linking each participant's results to independent building data such as moisture mapping, tape lifts, surface samples and air sampling, rather than relying on symptoms or a diagnosis that may itself include VCS.
  • Prospective designs that test people before and after remediation, with comparison groups whose buildings were not remediated.
  • Adaptive threshold methods and repeated presentations, which reduce guessing and measurement noise.2

The Visual Contrast Sensitivity test has been designed with this agenda in mind. Its published research plan links vision results to building assessment data through a job number, runs a normative and test–retest study, and then builds an exposome dataset combining vision results with spore-trap, tape-lift and settle-plate findings.30 All of this sits within a framework that keeps results on the user's own device unless they choose to share them.
